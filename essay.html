01 — Position

The claim, and the concession

Most interface really is dissolving. The disagreement is about what's left.

Let me start with where this came from and what I actually want to argue. I listened to Syntax #1029 with Nathan Sobo of Zed, about Delta, and one part of it sat down uncomfortably close to something I have been circling for months. The interesting move is not that a model writes files for you. It is that the conversation, the fine-grained evolution of the code, the decisions and the collaboration are treated as part of the actual development history — rather than being flattened into Git snapshots and pull requests after the fact. As Sobo puts it, conversations are becoming, in effect, the source our code comes out of; the code is the downstream artefact.

Before disagreeing with anything, I want to concede more than is comfortable. Sobo also argues that as language gets better we will need much less user interface, and he is largely right. Most of the chrome built over the last fifteen years exists because software could not understand a sentence. Configuration panels, wizards, advanced-search forms, the seventeen-field ticket dialog — these are not interfaces, they are parsers made of widgets. Language eats all of them, and good riddance. My objection is only about the remainder, the part that does not dissolve, which I think has been mischaracterised.

02 — Distinction

Intent and state are different jobs

Language is superb at one and weak at the other, in both directions.

That remainder rests on a distinction worth making carefully. An interface does at least two things: it lets you express intent, and it makes system state perceptible. Language is extraordinary at the first. It is not automatically superior at the second, and it is frequently terrible at it.

Put the three channels on those two axes and none of them reaches the top right. Language scores high on intent and low on perception; direct manipulation is the mirror image; visual structure carries state well but cannot receive a novel instruction. The answer is a composition, not a winner.

Figure 01 · what each channel is good at
Makes state perceptible → Expresses intent →

Read the corners. No channel occupies the top right — which is why the answer is a composition, not a winner.

The reason is not taste but the difference between a serial process and a parallel one. Certain visual features — colour, orientation, size, motion — are registered preattentively, across the whole field at once, without search (). Read a list to find the odd item and you are running a serial scan whose cost grows with list length. Code the same item by colour and the cost is close to flat. You can test this on yourself: find one marked token among forty-eight, once uncoded and once coloured, and the second search is typically several times faster although nothing about the content changed.

The same asymmetry runs in the input direction. Language is a poor channel for spatial and continuous adjustment. "Move that block up two, no, one" is not a superior interface to dragging it — pointing has a known, modellable cost that describing does not (). Direct manipulation is not a legacy affordance we tolerate until models improve. It is a faster channel for a class of instruction that language encodes badly.

The future may not be less UI.
It may be less fixed UI.

Which might not be a disagreement at all. If you already think interfaces get generated on demand, then less fixed UI is your position too, and I have written several hundred words to arrive where you started. The narrower claim I want to defend is that whatever gets generated has to be a frame, and framing is an old, well-studied and largely unsolved problem.

03 — Mechanism

Why the top-ten list beats the paragraph

Not stupidity. Chunking, and who is made to do it.

Before going near literary theory, it is worth admitting something about format. Nobody defends the listicle and everybody reads it faster, and the reason is not stupidity. Working memory holds something like four chunks, not seven (), and prose makes the reader do the chunking; a list has done it for them. Signalling — headings, numbering, emphasis — reliably improves comprehension of the same underlying content (), and eye-tracking shows people do not read pages so much as sweep them ().

Take the five failures listed later in this piece and write them twice at the same length: once as a flowing sentence, once as a numbered list. The list is not better writing. It is pre-chunked, so the reader spends their four slots on the argument instead of on parsing it.

Instrument 02 · same claims, two formats matched length
A long agent thread fails in several related ways: you cannot cite turn forty because nothing is addressable; a decision revised late looks identical to the stale one it replaced; tool calls are rendered at the same weight as reasoning; resolved stretches never close; and the diff, once produced, no longer carries the reasons that produced it.

04 — Evidence

Colour is a convention we acquired

The preference outruns the effect size. That gap is the finding.

There is a case close to home that shows how these devices actually get established. Developers have a documented fetish for colouring the terminal, and it is worth asking how much of that is measured benefit and how much is acquired convention, because the answer is more interesting than the fetish.

The evidence is real but modest. An eye-tracking study found syntax highlighting significantly reduced comprehension time — around eight seconds on average — with the effect shrinking as programming experience grew (). An earlier experiment found no significant benefit for visual search at all, and its participants preferred the coloured code anyway ().

That gap is the point. The preference outruns the effect size, which means this is not a raw perceptual win. It is a convention — a way of reading that a professional culture acquired, trained on, and now cannot comfortably work without. Media do not merely carry text; they teach a way of perceiving it, and that training is historical, not natural. Which is why the reading habits of someone raised on books are genuinely not those of someone raised on feeds, and why "just put it in prose" is not a neutral default. It is one convention among several, and currently the least equipped one.

05 — History

We read with borrowed apparatus

Eight centuries of accumulation, and then a cliff.

If conventions are acquired, they have a history, and that history is not flattering to us. Every framing device we use was invented at a particular moment, for a particular medium, and then inherited. None is obvious. All had to be built.

Count the framing devices available in each medium and the shape is unmistakable. A glossed manuscript page around 1200 has roughly six: marginalia, interlinear gloss, rubrication, running head, decorated initials, quire marks. Print adds pagination, the index, the footnote, the colophon — about nine. The nineteenth-century newspaper reaches eleven, the terminal drops back to seven, the graphical web climbs to thirteen. The feed falls to five. The agent thread has two: speaker label and timestamp.

Figure 02 · framing devices available in the medium

Read that sequence backwards and something uncomfortable shows up. Apparatus accumulated steadily for eight centuries — gloss, rubrication, the paragraph, the title page, the footnote, the column, the headline, the link, the syntax colour, the panel — and then, in the medium where we now do our most complex thinking, it collapsed. A five-hundred-message agent thread has fewer framing devices than a thirteenth-century glossed page.

06 — Theory

Textrahmen

Frames stopped leaning on an inherited order and started constituting one.

There is a literature on exactly this, and it is older than the problem. A book by Till Dembeck called Texte rahmen — roughly, framing texts — studies the paratextual frames around literary works in the second half of the eighteenth century (). Title pages, prefaces, dedications, footnotes, editorial apologies. His argument is that these edges are not decoration: they are where a text is bound into a cultural and communicative context. The frame is what makes a text receivable at all.

His historical finding is the part that should interest anyone building agent threads. Framing shifts. In the older model, the frame's job is to attach the text to a pre-existing order the reader already inhabits — a metaphysics, a genre, a moral world. In the newer model, the frame stops leaning on that order and starts constituting one: it embeds the text in a world of its own.

That is precisely the shift Delta is making, in a different medium. Today an AI thread is framed by a pre-existing order it did not create: the repository, the branch, the commit, the issue tracker. The conversation is an accessory to those and inherits its meaning from them. Saying conversation is the source removes that scaffolding. The thread now has to constitute its own world — and it needs its own frames to do it.

Dembeck's cases are the ones that manage the transition without a clean break: Wieland's Agathon, Sterne's Tristram Shandy, Jean Paul. Sterne in particular is a book composed almost entirely of its own framing devices, digressions and apparatus, published in instalments — a long thread with terrible navigation and a strong authorial voice. I feel we have all read this thread.

The third point in Dembeck has the sharpest engineering consequence. The frame asserts the unity of the text while making that unity visibly dependent on reception. Unity is not a property the text has; it is produced by the framing, for a reader. Which means there is no canonical structure of a thread. There are frames, and each constitutes a different unity for a different reader — which is why multiple live views over one context stops being a nice-to-have and becomes the only position consistent with how framing works.

07 — Diagnosis

Where the context currently lives

Outside the artefact. The design job is moving it in.

Genette gives us the vocabulary for saying where the problem sits (). He splits paratext in two. Peritext is the material inside the object: title, preface, chapter heads, marginalia, notes. Epitext is everything outside it: interviews, letters, reviews, the author explaining himself afterwards.

Picture the artefact — a diff, a document, a decision — as a box. Today the reasoning that produced it sits outside that box, scattered across a chat window, a Slack thread, a closed tab and somebody's memory. The conversion Delta proposes is positional: bring that apparatus inside the box, as a thread head above the artefact, a decision log beside it and a colophon beneath it, so it travels with the work.

Figure 03 · the conversion Delta proposes

The design job in one move. Not storage — we already store the thread. The conversion is positional.

Right now, the reasoning around your code is epitext. It sits in a chat window, a Slack thread, someone's head, a closed tab, and the commit message is a two-line gesture at it. What Delta proposes is a conversion: make the epitext peritext. That conversion is the whole design job, and it is not a storage problem — storing the thread is easy, we already do. The problem is that five hundred messages with no framing apparatus is not memory. It is sediment.

At five messages, chronology is enough. At fifty, topics emerge. At five hundred, you are not looking at a chat. You are looking at a project.

So the answer is to grow a project-management structure out of the chat: tasks, boards, owners, status — the conversation maturing into a tracker.

I thought that for a while, and it was the small version. A board is one frame, and not a very interesting one. The general move is framing itself: the same material bound into different unities, for different readers, at different moments — without a copy, an export, or a second tool that immediately starts drifting from the first.

08 — Specification

Five failures, five devices

Theory pays rent. None of these needs a model.

Theory earns its place by paying rent, so here is the rent: five failures I hit in practice in long agent threads, each with the paratextual device that answers it. What they need is a document model in which a message is not the only first-class object.

01
No addressability.

The device is a thread head.

You cannot cite turn 40. There is no stable handle for any span, so you re-paste instead of referring. A thread needs a preface: goal, constraints, and named linkable regions — the way a book has a title page and numbered chapters.

02
No supersession.

The device is a supersession mark.

A decision revised at turn 40 renders identically to the stale one at turn 12. Both are just messages, and retrieval and humans are both poisoned by it. Scholarly editions solved this: strike it, keep it visible, point at what replaced it. Delete nothing.

03
Apparatus crowds out argument.

The device is register separation.

Tool calls, diffs and logs render at the same visual weight as reasoning, so the argument drowns in its own machinery. This is what footnotes are for: keep the apparatus, demote it, expand on demand.

04
Nothing ever closes.

The device is a segment frame.

Resolved stretches stay open forever at full length. A thread should be able to say: turns 12–31 resolved the auth question, here is the result, collapsed. A chapter that has ended should look like it has ended.

05
The diff forgets its reasons.

The device is a colophon.

Artefact and conversation drift apart the moment they are separated. Every produced thing wants a colophon: what this was made from, by whom, after which decisions, with which questions still open.

09 — Demonstration

What this page is doing

The argument, executed rather than described.

The controls at the top of this page are not settings, and they divide the work between the two halves of this piece deliberately. The animation depicts: it can show ancestry, causality and time, and it is allowed to illustrate because that is what an animation is for. This page demonstrates. Its frames are real, its apparatus is real, and the claim that nothing is duplicated is checkable rather than asserted. Each artefact makes the argument it can actually make.

The apparatus control replays eight centuries in nine steps, on text that never changes: paragraphs, spacing, glosses, headings, editorial hierarchy — and then three steps the page had to earn rather than inherit. Hypertext breaks the scroll, so every claim becomes a place you can name. Layering trades length for depth: each claim carries its thesis and folds its argument away beneath it. And the last step returns the whole essay to a single opening.

The last step is the one I would defend hardest, and it is the oldest idea in the piece. German idealism has a word for understanding that arrives whole rather than in sequence: Anschauung — intuition in the literal sense, grasping something in a single view. It is what a good diagram does and a long document cannot. The mind map you draw the night before an examination works because the whole terrain is available at once; the trouble is that you can only draw it after you have read everything.

So the final station is not a diagram. It is the page from station 01, come back at the end to hold what nine sections held. An opening: the argument on the recto in section order, what grounds it on the verso, and any line opening its own gloss at the foot. Load-bearing claims set larger, concessions in italic beneath them, the superseded claim struck and still carrying a pointer to what replaced it — which is the apparatus of a scholarly edition doing exactly the job it was built for, on an essay about agent threads. Every region is derived from the same attributes the frames use. Nothing was placed by hand. If the codex could not carry this, the argument would be in trouble; it turns out it can.

Thread view is the one I would look at first, if only because it is embarrassing. It is where this came from: a podcast, voice memos full of transcription damage, a wrong turn, and a book from a seminar I took a long time ago. The essay is the polished frame. The thread is what was actually going on. Neither is the real version.

Conversation becomes the source of structured work.
Code is one downstream artefact. The interface is another.

If you are building this — and Delta is closer to it than anything else I have seen — the thing I would push on is that the frame is not a view layer you add later. It decides whether five hundred messages are a memory or a landfill. There is a literature on this. It is two hundred and fifty years old, mostly in German, and unexpectedly about you.
